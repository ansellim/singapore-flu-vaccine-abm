# Vaccination allocation literature evidence report

Literature scout report, 18 November 2026. Peer-reviewed research only. Searches used PubMed and scholarly publisher/repository searches. Four papers below had Methods, Results, and Discussion inspected, beyond abstracts. This is an evidence aid. No code was developed. Full-text XML retrieval was used solely to read publications.

## 1. Local historical vaccination baseline

Ang LW, Cutter J, James L, Goh KT. Factors associated with influenza vaccine uptake in older adults living in the community in Singapore. *Epidemiology and Infection*. 2017;145:775-786. [DOI 10.1017/S0950268816002491](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0950268816002491). [PMID 27927253](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27927253/). [Full text](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/epidemiology-and-infection/article/factors-associated-with-influenza-vaccine-uptake-in-older-adults-living-in-the-community-in-singapore/1A166D368366654B7B8F7158A3443390).

Read Materials and Methods, Results, Discussion, including limitations. NHSS 2013 two-stage stratified household survey; weighted cross-sectional analysis of 3,700 community-dwelling residents aged at least 50; self-reported vaccination in preceding year. Coverage was 15.2% overall, 14.6% at 50-64 and 17.0% in older adults. Study eligibility and analysis use age 65 or older, although one discussion sentence prints >65. Regular GP access associated with uptake: adjusted OR 1.28 (95% CI 1.06-1.55). Self-report, selection/nonresponse, and cross-sectional confounding limit inference. Authors explicitly anticipate uptake changes following subsequent programs. This supports maintaining nonzero age-specific baseline vaccination and modeling additional doses. It cannot supply contemporary coverage, population-wide adult coverage, or causal uptake response to outreach.

## 2. Household direct versus indirect protection, influenza B

Tsang TK et al. Indirect protection from vaccinating children against influenza in households. *Nature Communications*. 2019;10:106. [DOI 10.1038/s41467-018-08036-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-08036-6). [PMID 30631062](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30631062/). [Full text PDF](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/266863/1/Content.pdf). [Full text XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6328591/fullTextXML).

Read complete Results, Discussion, Methods via Europe PMC XML. Hong Kong randomized child vaccine/placebo trial, 2009-2010; modeled 745 households, 2,564 participants with paired-serology information. Bayesian directed-graph model separates community introductions and household spread. Estimated susceptibility-reducing efficacy in vaccinated children: 71% (53%-83% interval; prose calls CI, Bayesian figure calls credible interval). For unvaccinated adult contacts, total infection relative probability was 0.95 (95% posterior predictive interval 0.90-0.99) with one child vaccinated and 0.93 (0.87-0.98) with all children vaccinated. External exposure dominated. Serologic measurement and ceiling effects, selected households containing children, and fixed community exposure limit transportability. Household-only vaccination effects do not establish population indirect effects. Supports explicit household/community settings and direct versus indirect protection sensitivity. Per-pair transmission was lower in larger households; occupancy alone is not a justified crowding multiplier.

## 3. Influenza A and age-dependent susceptibility

Tsang TK et al. Indirect Protection from Vaccinating Children against Influenza A Virus Infection in Households. *Viruses*. 2022;14:2097. [DOI 10.3390/v14102097](https://doi.org/10.3390/v14102097). [PMID 36298653](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36298653/). [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9610389/). [Full text XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9610389/fullTextXML).

Read Methods, Results, Discussion in full via XML. Hong Kong household cohort, 2009-2013, six A(H1N1)pdm09/H3N2 epidemics. Paired-serology infection and Bayesian directed-graph transmission model; vaccinated participants' infection status treated as missing. Susceptibility varied by age and antibody titer. Household transmission accounted for 10% (95% credible interval 7%-13%) of cases. Vaccine protection values 30%, 50%, 70% were simulation assumptions, not estimated adult vaccine effectiveness. With assumed 70% efficacy and every child vaccinated, unvaccinated household contacts' total infection relative probabilities were 0.91-0.94 across epidemics. Larger households again had lower per-pair transmission. Children-containing households, serology error, fixed community risk, and absence of between-household feedback constrain application. Supports distinct baseline age susceptibility and vaccine susceptibility effects; do not encode age effects twice or transplant pediatric efficacy to adults aged at least 65.

## 4. Allocation, transmissibility, and intervention timing

Bansal S, Pourbohloul B, Meyers LA. A Comparative Analysis of Influenza Vaccination Programs. *PLoS Medicine*. 2006;3:e387. [DOI 10.1371/journal.pmed.0030387](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0030387). [PMID 17020406](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17020406/). [Full text](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.0030387).

Read Methods and entire Results/Discussion. Vancouver demographic contact network, approximately 257,000 people; school, work, household, and hospital contacts; SIR simulations checked analytical predictions. Compared allocation strategies at equal overall vaccine coverage, principally 13%. Vaccination modeled as complete protection in an age-specific fraction. Transmission-focused strategies minimized infections; elderly protection and mortality rankings could reverse as transmissibility increased. Delayed vaccination and multiple introductions weakened transmission-focused advantages. Delay was defined by cumulative proportion already infected, not days. Historic efficacy/coverage assumptions, static Canadian network, and different target groups prevent direct numerical transfer. Supports testing transmissibility, introductions, and pre-wave versus reactive campaigns. Does not establish that broad adult random vaccination has the effects of vaccinating children or the most connected people. An infection-only ABM cannot identify a mortality or economic optimum.

## Implications that are modeling judgments, not estimated evidence

Use existing coverage/no additional doses, equal additional administered doses allocated across adults aged at least 18, and the same additional doses prioritized to ages at least 65. Explicitly handle saturation when the older eligible pool is exhausted. Equal doses answer allocation efficiency; equal outreach spending would require separate uptake and cost assumptions.

Model vaccination as a leaky reduction in susceptibility only if that mechanism is explicitly assumed. Clinical symptomatic-influenza VE is not necessarily susceptibility VE. None of these fully inspected papers identifies a contemporary Singapore susceptibility-VE pair for adults aged 18-64 versus at least 65. Any chosen pair needs an uncertainty grid, not false empirical precision. Do not add vaccine effects on infectiousness without justification.

A campaign before the first modeled introductions isolates preventive allocation; a reactive campaign after outbreak detection is a separate timing sensitivity. Day 7-20 vaccination, 14-day protection lag, and introductions on day 35 are operational assumptions. They describe an illustrative wave and should not imply Singapore has no interwave circulation. The 14-day lag itself requires an additional peer-reviewed source if presented as empirically established.

Separate total household occupancy, age composition, and space/contact intensity. Larger families create more potential transmission partners even if each pair's risk decreases. Neither Tsang paper estimates an occupancy-per-floor-area effect. Retain structural crowding sensitivity rather than claiming an empirically calibrated positive slope.
